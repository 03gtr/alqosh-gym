Supplement shelves promise a lot. The truth is simpler: most of your results come from consistent training, enough food and protein, and good sleep. Supplements are optional, and at best they add a small extra on top of those basics.

## Food first

The International Olympic Committee (IOC) consensus statement on dietary supplements (2018) supports a "food first" approach: build a good diet before thinking about supplements. A pill or powder cannot fix poor sleep, skipped meals or an inconsistent training plan.

## The few with good evidence

For performance, only a small number of supplements have strong evidence. For gym members, the main two are **caffeine** and **creatine** — see our dedicated articles on each. The IOC statement also lists a few others, such as beetroot juice (nitrate), beta-alanine and sodium bicarbonate, which are mostly relevant to specific competitive sports.

Health supplements such as vitamin D or iron can be useful when there is a proven deficiency. That should be confirmed with a blood test and a doctor — not guessed.

## Protein powder is just food

The ISSN sports nutrition review (Kerksick and colleagues, 2018) treats protein powder as a convenient way to help meet your daily protein needs. It is not magic, and eggs, chicken, fish, laban, cheese and lentils do the same job.

## The contamination risk

The IOC warns that supplements are not regulated as strictly as medicines, and some products have been found to contain substances not listed on the label — including stimulants and substances banned in sport. If you use supplements:

- Choose products tested by an independent third party.
- Be extra careful if you compete in a drug-tested sport.
- Be suspicious of products that promise dramatic results.

## Say no to "fat burners"

No pill burns fat for you. Products sold as "fat burners" often rely on high doses of stimulants and may contain hidden ingredients, so the risks can outweigh any small effect. Fat loss comes from a sustainable calorie deficit, training and patience.

## Questions to ask before you buy

1. Do I actually need it?
2. Is there good evidence it works for my goal?
3. Is it safe for me, and has it been tested by a third party?
4. Have I already fixed the basics: training, food and sleep?

If you take medication, have a chronic condition, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or are under 18, talk to a doctor or registered dietitian before taking any supplement.

## Bottom line

- Food, training and sleep come first; supplements are optional.
- Only a few supplements, such as caffeine and creatine, have strong evidence.
- Contamination is a real risk — choose third-party tested products.
- Skip "fat burners".
