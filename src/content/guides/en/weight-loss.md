Losing weight is not a race. The approach that lasts is the one that fits your life: food that keeps you satisfied, movement you enjoy, enough sleep, and patience with slow change. This guide walks you through the basics step by step. It is general education, not medical advice or a personal plan.

## Energy balance and your daily needs

Over time, your weight changes according to the difference between the energy you eat and drink and the energy your body uses. When you eat a little less than you need, your body draws on its stores and your weight comes down gradually.

Two terms help you understand your needs:

- **Basal metabolic rate (BMR):** the energy your body needs at rest — for breathing, your heart and brain, and keeping warm.
- **Total daily energy expenditure (TDEE):** your BMR plus the energy used for digestion, everyday movement and exercise.

Our [calorie calculator](/en/calculator/?goal=loss) estimates both and suggests an intake for weight loss. Any calculator is based on averages, so treat the result as a starting point, not an exact number.

The body is also not a simple calculator. Research by Hall and colleagues in The Lancet showed that your energy needs fall as your weight falls, so progress slows over time even if your habits stay the same. That is normal adaptation, not failure. Read more in our article on [energy balance](/en/articles/energy-balance/).

## A moderate deficit, not harsh restriction

For weight loss, the calculator suggests an intake only about 10–20% below your TDEE — a **moderate** deficit. That may sound slow, but it is deliberate:

- Harsh restriction increases hunger and tiredness and is hard to keep up.
- It makes holding on to muscle harder and drags down your training.
- It often leads to a cycle of strict dieting followed by overeating.

A moderate deficit lets you eat normal meals with your family, train with good energy, and keep going for months. You don't need extreme fasting, "detox" plans or supplements that promise to burn fat.

## Protein protects your muscle

When you eat less than you need, your body needs a reason and the materials to keep its muscle. The reason is resistance training; the materials come from protein. For people who exercise, the International Society of Sports Nutrition suggests about **1.4–2.0 g of protein per kg of body weight per day**, spread across your meals.

Protein also helps you feel full. Include a source at every meal: chicken, fish, eggs, lean meat, laban, cheese, lentils, chickpeas. Learn more in our article on [protein](/en/articles/protein/).

## Fibre and vegetables

Vegetables, fruit, beans and whole grains fill your plate and keep you full for longer with a moderate amount of calories. Systematic reviews published in The Lancet in 2019 linked higher fibre intake with better health and suggested aiming for at least 25–29 g a day.

Simple steps:

- Fill about half your plate with vegetables or salad.
- Choose bulgur, wholemeal bread and oats more often than refined options.
- Add lentils, chickpeas and beans to your meals.
- Increase fibre gradually and drink enough water with it.

You'll find ready-made ideas in our [weight-loss meals](/en/nutrition/meals/?goal=loss).

## Everyday movement

Movement outside the gym — walking, taking the stairs, housework — is a meaningful part of the energy you use each day. The World Health Organization advises adults to get **150–300 minutes of moderate activity per week** and to cut down on long periods of sitting.

Start from where you are: if you walk very little now, add a short walk after meals, then build up the time gradually. Every bit of movement counts.

## Strength training and cardio

**Resistance training** is the most important way to protect your muscle while losing weight, and WHO recommends it on **2 or more days a week**. Stronger muscles make daily life easier and your body more capable. Build your plan with our [workout builder](/en/workouts/?goal=fat-loss#builder).

**Cardio** — brisk walking, cycling, swimming — supports heart health and adds to the energy you use. The American College of Sports Medicine position stand notes that regular physical activity helps with weight loss and is especially helpful for preventing weight regain. Pick something you enjoy; consistency matters more than intensity.

## Sleep, stress and recovery

Short sleep and ongoing stress can increase hunger and cravings and sap your energy for training. Adults are advised to sleep **7 or more hours per night on a regular basis**.

To ease stress, try a relaxed walk, slow breathing, or time with family and friends. Give your body rest days between hard sessions — progress is built during recovery. Read more in our [recovery guide](/en/learn/recovery/).

## Consistency over perfection

There is no perfect week. Celebrations, family gatherings and tiring days will come, and that is a normal part of life. What makes the difference is what you do on most days, not on any single day.

- If you eat more than planned, simply return to your plan at the next meal — no harsh "making up for it".
- Start with one or two habits and add more once they feel settled.
- Be kind to yourself: slow progress is still progress.

## Track progress in more than one way

The scale alone doesn't tell the whole story. Daily weight moves up and down with water, salt and food in your gut. So keep an eye on several signs:

- **Weekly average weight:** weigh yourself under the same conditions on several mornings and compare the average week to week, rather than single days.
- **Waist measurement:** use a tape measure the same way every couple of weeks.
- **How your clothes fit:** notice how your usual clothes feel.
- **Strength:** are your weights or reps improving in training?
- **Photos:** take them in the same light and from the same angle now and then.

Keep your notes on the [progress page](/en/progress/). If your average stays flat for several weeks, check your consistency first, then make one small change — and ask your coach at the gym when you need help.

## When to see a doctor or dietitian

This guide is for education only and does not diagnose or treat anything. Talk with your doctor or a registered dietitian before changing how you eat if you:

- Have a health condition such as diabetes, heart disease or kidney disease.
- Take medication that affects weight, appetite or blood sugar.
- Are pregnant or breastfeeding.
- Have a history of eating disorders, or feel that food and weight cause you a lot of worry.
- Notice weight loss without a clear reason.

Asking for help is a smart step, not a weakness.
