Once the beginner gains slow down, progress needs more thought. This guide covers the main training variables experienced lifters use to keep improving. It is general education: there is no single "best" programme, results vary from person to person, and nothing here replaces individual advice from a qualified coach — or a doctor if you have an injury or a medical condition.

## Who this guide is for

This guide is for you if you have trained consistently for a good while, you know the main lifts well, and simply adding a little weight every session no longer works. If you are still learning the basics, start with our [beginner guide](/en/beginner/) — it will serve you better.

## Progressive overload, revisited

The principle stays the same: to keep adapting, the training stimulus must gradually increase over time. For experienced lifters, it can come from several directions:

- More load on the bar.
- More reps at the same load.
- More sets (volume).
- Better technique and range of motion with the same weight.
- More work in the same time (density).

The American College of Sports Medicine describes increasing the load by about **2–10%** once you can exceed your target reps by one or two. As you advance, progress becomes slower and less linear, so expect to measure it over weeks and months rather than session by session.

## Volume: sets per muscle per week

Volume is often counted as the number of hard sets per muscle group per week. A 2017 meta-analysis by Schoenfeld and colleagues, pooling 15 studies, found a **graded dose-response relationship**: higher weekly volumes were associated with greater muscle growth. When the authors grouped volumes into fewer than 5, 5–9 and 10 or more sets per muscle per week, the differences only showed a *trend*, not a clear-cut result.

How to use this cautiously:

- More volume can help, but only up to the amount you can recover from.
- The studies were limited in number and length, so they do not define a perfect number for you.
- Increase sets gradually and watch your performance, sleep and joints.

## Intensity: how heavy

Intensity usually means load relative to your maximum. For advanced lifters aiming at muscle growth, ACSM describes using a wide range of loads (from 1 to 12 repetition maximum) in a periodized way, with most emphasis on the **6–12 rep** zone; for maximal strength it emphasises heavier loads of about **1–6 reps**. Most experienced programmes mix heavier and lighter days or blocks rather than using one rep range forever.

## Effort: RIR and RPE

Effort tells you how close to failure a set is. Two practical tools:

- **RIR (reps in reserve):** how many more good reps you could have done.
- **RPE (rating of perceived exertion):** a 1–10 score of how hard the set was.

Helms and colleagues described an RIR-based RPE scale for lifting: **RPE 10 = no reps left, RPE 9 = 1 rep left, RPE 8 = 2 reps left**, and so on. This lets you adjust load day to day: if your planned set at RPE 8 feels like RPE 9.5, take some weight off. Most sets do not need to go to failure; keeping a rep or two in reserve on heavy compound lifts helps protect technique.

## Frequency: how often per muscle

Frequency is how often you train each muscle or lift per week. ACSM recommends about **2–3 days a week for novices, 3–4 for intermediate and 4–5 for advanced** lifters, with more advanced lifters often using splits. A useful way to think about it: frequency is a tool for spreading your weekly volume into sessions where every set is still good quality.

## Choosing a training split

Common options include:

- **Full body:** every session trains the whole body.
- **Upper / lower:** sessions alternate between upper-body and lower-body days.
- **Push / pull / legs:** pushing muscles, pulling muscles and legs each get their own day.
- **Body-part split:** one or two muscle groups per session.

No split is magic. Choose the one that fits your schedule, lets you recover, and you can stick with for months. Browse templates in our [workouts](/en/workouts/) section.

## Exercise selection

Build around compound lifts (squat, hinge, press, row, pull-down or pull-up) and add isolation exercises for specific muscles. Include both free weights and machines if they suit you. Keep your main lifts stable for long enough to measure progress, and rotate accessory exercises when they stop feeling productive or bother your joints. Our [exercises](/en/exercises/) section has technique notes for each movement.

## Training density

Density is how much work you do in a given time. Shorter rests and supersets (pairing non-competing exercises, such as a press with a row) can save time. But rest too short on heavy lifts can reduce performance; ACSM describes rests of about **3–5 minutes** for heavy strength work and about **1–2 minutes** for muscle-building work. Match your rest to the goal of the exercise.

## Deloads

A deload is a planned lighter period, often around a week, with reduced volume, load or both. It is used to manage accumulated fatigue and let you return fresh. Some lifters plan deloads regularly; others use them when performance stalls, motivation drops, or joints feel beaten up. There is no universal schedule; let your training log guide you.

## Recovery

Advanced training only works if you recover from it:

- **Sleep:** adults are advised to get **7 or more hours per night on a regular basis**.
- **Protein:** about **1.4–2.0 g per kg of body weight per day** for people who train (ISSN), spread across meals.
- **Enough total energy**, especially when chasing strength or muscle gain.
- **Stress management:** work, study and life stress all affect recovery.

## Technique under fatigue

As loads and volumes rise, technique tends to break down at the end of hard sets. Keep standards high: set a clear rule for when a rep "counts", film your top sets now and then, and end a set when form changes noticeably. Joint pain, sharp pain or numbness are reasons to stop and see a doctor or physiotherapist — not something to train through.

## Track, review, adjust

Keep a training log with exercises, loads, reps and RPE or RIR. Every few weeks, review it:

- Are your key lifts or reps going up?
- Are you recovering between sessions?
- Do your joints feel good?

If progress stalls, change one variable at a time — volume, intensity, frequency or exercise selection — so you can see what made the difference. When in doubt, ask a qualified coach to look at your plan.
